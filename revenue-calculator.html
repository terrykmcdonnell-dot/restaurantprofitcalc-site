How to calculate restaurant revenue

Restaurant revenue comes down to three numbers for each service: how many seats you have, how many times you fill them, and what each guest spends.

Weekly revenue = seats × table turns × trading days × average spend, for each service, plus takeaway and other sales

A table turn is one full use of your seats during a service. Serve 72 guests in a 60-seat room across a dinner service and you've turned the room 1.2 times. A figure below 1 means some seats stayed empty all night, which is normal for lunch in most suburban restaurants.

Work each service out separately. Lunch and dinner usually have very different turns and very different spend, and averaging them hides where the money actually comes from.

A worked example

The calculator above opens with this example. A 60-seat restaurant opens for lunch five days a week and dinner six nights, and does $1,200 a week in takeaway.

Lunch60 seats × 0.6 turns × 5 days × $28$5,040
Dinner60 seats × 1.2 turns × 6 nights × $55$23,760

Over 50 trading weeks that's $1.5 million a year, or $25,000 per seat. Dinner brings in nearly four of every five dollars, which is common, and it means a quiet month at dinner hurts far more than a quiet month at lunch.

The three ways to grow revenue

1. Lift average spend

Usually the fastest and cheapest lever, because it needs no extra customers. Adding $3 a head in the example is worth about $92,000 a year. Drinks, sides and desserts are where most of that comes from, and they tend to carry better margins than mains. Our guide to beverage profit margins covers why drinks matter so much.

2. Turn tables more often

Only works if there's demand you're turning away. If you're full at 7pm and empty at 9pm, an earlier first sitting or shorter table times can add a turn. If the room is half empty all night, the problem is demand, not turns.

3. Trade more sessions

Opening an extra lunch or another night adds revenue, but it also adds wages, power and wear. It only pays if the new session's sales cover its own costs. The break-even calculator helps you test that before you commit staff to it.

To see these levers clearly you need covers and spend broken down by service, week by week. Most modern POS systems report this automatically; a system like TouchBistro shows covers, average spend and table turns per service without you building a spreadsheet. Forecasting revenue for a new restaurant

The calculator works for a restaurant that hasn't opened yet, but be honest with the inputs. New owners almost always overestimate turns. A brand-new restaurant rarely trades at its long-run level in the first six to twelve months, and a plan that only works at full trade has no room for that ramp-up.

  • Run the numbers at the turns you expect, then again at 20% lower. If the lower figure doesn't cover your costs, the plan is fragile.
  • Base average spend on comparable restaurants nearby, not on your menu prices. Not every guest orders a starter and a drink.
  • Don't sign a lease on the strength of the optimistic case. Rent is locked in; revenue isn't. Why restaurants fail walks through what happens when revenue comes in 19% below plan.

Revenue isn't profit

A restaurant can take $1.5 million and still lose money. Revenue tells you the size of the business; food cost, labour and rent decide how much of it you keep. Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate restaurant revenue?

For each service, multiply seats by table turns by trading days to get covers, then multiply by average spend per head. Add the services together, plus takeaway and other sales.

What is a table turn?

One full use of your seats during a service. Guests served ÷ seats. 72 guests in 60 seats is 1.2 turns.

Which lever increases revenue fastest?

If you're full at peak times, average spend or an extra service. If you have empty seats, more covers. The table in the calculator shows the annual effect of each lever on your own numbers.

Should revenue include GST or sales tax?

No. Use spend excluding GST, VAT or sales tax. The tax isn't your money, and including it overstates everything that depends on revenue, including your cost percentages.

How do I find my actual table turns?

Take the covers your POS recorded for a service and divide by your seats. Do it for a few typical weeks rather than one; a single busy Saturday will flatter the figure.
