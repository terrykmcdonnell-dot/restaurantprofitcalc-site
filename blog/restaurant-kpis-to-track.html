The Restaurant KPIs Worth Tracking (And the Ones That Waste Your Time)

By Terry McDonnell — chartered accountant, former owner of seven fast-food franchises and a nine-year independent restaurant
Published August 2026  ·  8 min read

Modern POS systems will generate more reports than you could read in a week. Most operators respond by either drowning in them or ignoring them entirely, and the second group often does better — at least they're not mistaking activity for management.

A metric earns its place if it changes a decision. If you'd do exactly the same thing whatever the number said, stop tracking it.

The weekly four

These four take fifteen minutes together and catch nearly every developing problem.

1. Labour as a percentage of sales

Total wages ÷ revenue × 100. Benchmark 28–35%. Check it every week without exception — labour is the cost that drifts most quietly, and by the time a monthly P&L shows it you've already written four more rosters. Split kitchen from front of house; a well-controlled kitchen frequently masks an overstaffed floor.

2. Food and beverage cost percentage

Benchmark 28–35% for food, 18–25% for beverage, tracked separately. A weekly stock count is the accurate method; purchases ÷ revenue is the rough one. Rough weekly beats accurate monthly.

3. Prime cost

Food plus beverage plus total labour, as a percentage of revenue. Target under 65%, ideally under 60%. It's the single most important operating number because it combines your two largest controllable costs into one figure you can act on.

4. Revenue against forecast

Not just what you took, but what you took against what you expected. A week that hits budget and a week that misses by 15% require different responses, and you can only tell them apart if you wrote the expectation down first.

The 2% rule: if food cost or labour runs more than two percentage points above target in any week, investigate immediately. Don't wait to see whether it corrects itself. It rarely does.

Monthly metrics

Average spend per head

Revenue ÷ covers. It moves slowly, which is why weekly checking is noise. Falling average spend with steady covers usually means beverage attachment has slipped or staff have stopped suggesting extras — both fixable, neither visible in a revenue total.

Covers by day and service

The foundation of any sensible roster. Most rosters are built from habit; a month of cover counts by hour turns rostering into arithmetic and typically finds 2–3% of labour that nobody misses.

Contribution margin by dish

Price minus plate cost, in dollars, ranked. Combined with unit sales this is menu engineering, and it consistently identifies two or three popular dishes making almost nothing. Covered in more depth in our guide to pricing menu items.

The full P&L

Every cost line as a percentage of revenue, compared to last month and the same month last year. This is where crept overheads become obvious.

Worth knowing, rarely worth watching weekly

Table turn time. Matters enormously in high-volume rooms with a queue; matters very little if you aren't turning tables away.

RevPASH — revenue per available seat hour. Genuinely useful for deciding trading hours, since it exposes services that look busy but generate little. Overkill as a routine metric for a single site.

Online ratings. Watch the trend and read the content; ignore the decimal. Movement from 4.4 to 4.3 is noise. A pattern of complaints about the same thing is information.

Metrics that mislead

Gross revenue on its own. The most quoted number in hospitality and the least informative. Revenue is up 20% and you're making less money is an extremely common story.

Social media following. Unless you can trace bookings to it, it measures attention rather than trade.

Covers alone. More people spending less isn't progress. Always pair covers with average spend.

Food cost percentage in isolation. A falling food cost percentage can mean better buying — or that customers have shifted toward cheap, low-margin dishes. Read it alongside average spend and contribution margin.

Keep it on one page

Four numbers weekly, four monthly, on a single sheet with last period beside it and the benchmark beside that. Paper is fine.

The value isn't in the sophistication of the tracking. It's in looking at the same numbers on the same day every week, so that a small movement registers as unusual while it's still small enough to fix.
